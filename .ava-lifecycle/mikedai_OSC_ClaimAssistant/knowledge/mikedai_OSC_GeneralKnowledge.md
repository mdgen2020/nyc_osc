Question:
What are unclaimed funds, and is there a fee to claim them?
Answer:
Unclaimed funds are money that was lost or forgotten over time, such as old bank accounts, uncashed checks, stock certificates, and unused gift cards. After a period of inactivity, the money is treated as abandoned property and turned over to the New York State Comptroller's Office. There is no fee to claim it and no time limit to claim it. Searching for your name and filing a claim are free services.
Notes:
- If someone asks you to pay a fee to get your unclaimed funds, be cautious and use the official New York State Comptroller's website or phone number instead.

Role: Claimant
Task:
Search for unclaimed funds in your name or your business name.
Purpose:
Use this to find out whether the State is holding property that belongs to you before you file a claim.
Prerequisites:
- You are a current or former New York resident, or you have a connection to a New York business.
Procedure:
1. Go to the official unclaimed funds search on the Comptroller's website.
2. Enter your last name or your business name in the Last or Business Name field, then select Search.
3. To narrow the results, add your first name, city, and ZIP code.
4. Try other versions of your name, such as nicknames, common misspellings, and any previous addresses.
5. If you received a letter or email about unclaimed property, enter the Property ID from that notice and leave the other fields blank.
Outcome:
Exact matches appear first, followed by partial matches.
Exceptions / Rules:
- If the original holder told you your property was turned over to the State, it can take up to three months to appear in the search. If it is still missing after three months, you can mail a claim form instead.
- You can search for friends and family and use the Share button to let them know about a match.

Role: Claimant
Task:
File a claim for unclaimed property online.
Purpose:
Use this when a property is in your name and shows an address, or a business, that you have a connection to.
Prerequisites:
- The property is in your name and you connect to its address or reporting business.
- You are ready to provide your name, date of birth, contact information, and the mailing address where a check should go if approved.
Procedure:
1. In the search results, select Claim next to each property that belongs to you, then Continue to File a Claim.
2. Choose your relationship to the owner for each property. Choose Owner (Self) if the property is in your name.
3. Enter your own information, even if you are claiming for someone else.
4. Review your claim, then sign it electronically by checking the box and typing your first and last name, and select Submit.
Outcome:
You may be asked for documents that prove ownership. Check your email for next steps.
Exceptions / Rules:
- If you have co-owners, you can file by yourself.
- U.S. citizens are asked for a Social Security Number to file online. If you prefer not to provide it, you can mail your claim instead.
- To protect you from fraud, the amount cannot be shared until your claim has been reviewed.

Question:
Can I file a claim for someone else, such as my child or a family member?
Answer:
Yes, if you are one of the relationships listed here. You can submit a claim for another person if you are the owner's parent, custodian, guardian, conservator, trustee, power of attorney, or legal representative. When you file, enter your own information, not the owner's, and choose your relationship to the owner. You will need to prove your connection to the owner, for example with a child's birth certificate or adoption order for a minor, proof of your own age, and a court document or signed legal paper that gives you authority.
Notes:
- These claims can need more documents and take longer than usual.
- A team member can help you understand which documents your situation needs.

Question:
What if the owner of the property has passed away?
Answer:
I'm sorry for your loss. A claim for a deceased owner or an estate follows its own rules, and it usually needs extra documentation and can take longer than a standard claim. The Comptroller's website has a page called Claims for Deceased Owners and Estates that explains who is eligible and how to get a death certificate if one is requested. A team member can walk you through the documents for your situation.
Notes:
- If a check or letter was sent in the name of someone who has passed away, notify the Comptroller's office so the check can be voided. Depending on your relationship, you may be able to claim the funds.
- Do not share a Social Security number over chat or voice.

Question:
What happens after I submit a claim, and when will I hear back?
Answer:
After you submit a claim online, check your email. You should receive a confirmation email within 48 hours that tells you whether more information is needed. If the email says no further action is needed, a claim should be processed within 30 days. If the email includes a claim form and a list of documents, complete the form and send what is listed. After your additional information is received, allow up to 90 days for review.
Notes:
- Claims for someone else, a deceased owner, or stock can take longer.
- If you do not receive a confirmation email within 48 hours, check your junk or spam folder, then contact the Comptroller's office.
- These are general timeframes, not a promised date.

Question:
What proof of an old address can I send?
Answer:
If your current address is different from the address on the property, send proof that you are connected to the old address. Acceptable proof includes a driver's license or non-driver photo ID showing the old address; a birth, marriage, or death certificate that shows it; a passport showing it; bills and statements such as bank, utility, medical, or credit card; tax records like W-2 and 1099 forms, school, insurance, medical, or military records; legal papers like a divorce decree or mortgage; or a postmarked envelope.
Notes:
- Tax records such as W-2s and 1099s showing an old address are a common choice.
- If you cannot provide proof, send a written explanation, for example that you never lived there or it has been too long since you moved.

Question:
What counts as proof of ownership or proof of a name change?
Answer:
Proof of ownership includes copies of bank books, passbooks, or bank or mutual fund statements; certificates such as stocks, bonds, or bond coupons; uncashed checks such as money orders, gift certificates, or traveler's checks; and insurance policies. Proof of a name change includes a marriage certificate, adoption papers, a divorce decree, or court papers.
Notes:

- If you do not have the requested documents, send a written explanation instead.

Question:
What documents prove I can claim on someone else's behalf?
Answer:
To claim for another person, prove your connection to the owner. For a child under 18, send a copy of the child's birth certificate or a legal adoption order. Send proof of your own age, such as a birth certificate, driver's license, or other government ID that shows your birth date. Also send court or signed legal documents that give you authority, such as a court order, letters of guardianship or conservatorship, a trust agreement, a power of attorney, or legal representative papers. A guardian may also need a notarized statement that the owner is still living and the guardianship remains in full force.
Notes:
- If a death certificate is requested, the Comptroller's website explains how to obtain one.
- A team member can help you confirm which documents apply.

Question:
What documents does a business need to claim property?
Answer:
To sign for an organization, send proof of authorization, such as a notarized claim form with the corporate seal, a notarized statement from an officer or partner authorizing the signer, a recent dated cancelled business check, or a certificate of sole proprietorship or DBA. For a name change, merger, or acquisition, send items like a Certificate of Change, articles of incorporation, or merger agreements. For a business that closed, dissolved, or was sold, send a certificate of dissolution, the sales agreement, bankruptcy papers, or a notarized statement explaining what happened.
Notes:
- Business claims often need a specialist, and a team member can help you choose the right documents.

Task:
Submit your requested documents online.
Purpose:
Use this when your claim needs documents to prove ownership. Uploading online is the fastest and most secure way to continue your claim.
Prerequisites:
- Your Claim ID, which is in your claim form or email.
- The email address you gave when you submitted your claim.
- Electronic copies of your documents.
Procedure:
1. Scan each paper document or take a clear photo with your phone. Combine multiple pages into one file when you can.
2. Save each file as a PDF, PNG, JPG, or TIF that is under 10 MB. Files cannot be password protected. File names can use only letters, numbers, dashes, and underscores, up to 100 characters.
3. Make sure every part of the document is visible and readable.
4. If a signed claim form is requested, print it, sign it by hand, then scan or photograph it. Electronic signatures are not accepted.
5. Upload each file in its own field, choose a document type, confirm the verification checkbox, and submit. Stay on the page until you see the Upload Success screen.
Outcome:
You see your upload details confirming the documents were submitted.
Exceptions / Rules:
- If you have more than five files, submit the first five, then repeat.
- For files over 10 MB, save them as compressed PDFs.
- Documents are never collected by voice or by chat. This assistant can only send you to the secure upload page.

Question:
What happens when my claim is approved, and how will I be paid?
Answer:
When a claim is approved, the Comptroller's office emails you the payment details, including the amount. Your check is mailed to the address on your claim form within 30 days. If your claim included more than one property, you will usually receive one check for the total. The check arrives in a perforated envelope, like a W-2 or paycheck, from the Office of the New York State Comptroller. Direct deposit and prepaid cards are not available at this time.
Notes:
- If your property earned interest, payment may include interest for up to five years, and some payments may be subject to federal and state taxes. This assistant cannot give tax advice.
